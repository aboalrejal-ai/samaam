# Saudi CT, Iodinated Contrast, and Healthcare AI Governance: Primary-Source Knowledge Base

## Research standard and controlling findings

This review is current to **29 August 2026**. I prioritized Saudi primary sources from SFDA, NRRC, SDAIA/PDPL, and official Saudi health-information portals, then current primary clinical/governance sources from ACR, ESUR, WHO, ITU, IAEA, and ICRP. Where the public Saudi source could not be retrieved at provision level, I have **not substituted a blog, vendor document, law-firm summary, Scribd copy, or secondary clinical paper for the missing primary text**.

There is one necessary limitation on the requested “verbatim” field: each `content` field below contains a **short verbatim excerpt** from the identified provision rather than reproducing long copyrighted passages. The exact section/page and direct official source are supplied so the complete provision can be checked in the source document.

The most important Saudi-device conclusion is unusually clear. Current SFDA guidance says standalone software that drives or influences a hardware medical device and itself has a medical purpose can qualify as SaMD; SFDA’s MDMA classification rules separately classify devices controlling, monitoring, or directly influencing diagnostic/therapeutic ionizing-radiation equipment as **Class C**. Patient-specific diagnosis/therapy decision-support software starts at **Class B under Rule 11**, escalating to Class C or D according to the potential consequence of the decision. Current SFDA digital-health guidance additionally requires defined healthcare-professional roles and safeguards for human review and intervention. citeturn26view0turn27view0turn32view0turn32view2

For iodinated IV contrast, the strongest current numeric source captured is the **ACR Manual on Contrast Media 2026**. For stable renal function, ACR states that IV iodinated contrast is not an independent nephrotoxic risk at **eGFR ≥45 mL/min/1.73 m²** and is either not nephrotoxic or only rarely so at **30–44**; its evidence review says **30 mL/min/1.73 m²** is the best-supported threshold if a threshold is used. Prophylactic volume expansion is indicated for AKI or severe CKD with **eGFR <30**, is not routinely indicated for stable eGFR ≥30, and may be considered individually at 30–44 when other high-risk circumstances exist. citeturn21view0turn22view1turn22view2turn23view0

For metformin, ACR 2026 permits continuation when there is no AKI and eGFR is **≥30**; with AKI, **eGFR <30**, or certain arterial catheter procedures carrying renal embolic risk, metformin is stopped at or before the procedure, withheld for 48 hours, and restarted after renal function is reassessed. citeturn22view3turn23view3

**Crucially, those ACR numbers are not Saudi statutory cut-offs.** After searches of the public MoH site in English and Arabic for iodinated contrast, CT contrast, renal screening, eGFR/GFR, hydration, AKI/CIN, and metformin, I did **not locate a national Saudi MoH clinical protocol containing explicit 30 or 45 mL/min/1.73 m² thresholds**. Accordingly, any claim that “Saudi law requires eGFR 30/45” is **UNVERIFIED** on the primary-source record assembled here.

The current ESUR document is **ESUR Contrast Media Safety Committee Guidelines 2025**, released in November 2025; its renal chapter is pp. 15–18. The current PDF itself was verified, but the numeric renal paragraphs were not successfully extracted in the final primary-source capture, so I do **not** represent older ESUR values as if they were verified 2025 values. ESUR version 10.0 from 2018 is retained below only as an explicitly historical record, including its renal-function/contrast-volume ratio. citeturn43view0turn41search3

The Saudi DRL situation requires similar caution. A document titled **National Diagnostic Reference Levels (MDS-G008), version 2.0, dated 26 October 2022** is consistently identified in the scholarly regulatory literature, but I did not obtain the primary SFDA MDS-G008 PDF during this source capture. Therefore **no Saudi CT DRL number is reported here as verified**. citeturn42search2

The historical **Medical Devices Interim Regulation** is real: an SFDA primary announcement identifies it as having been issued under Board Decree **1-8-1429 dated 27 December 2008**, and SFDA also held an official launch workshop concerning the regime. The actual historical regulation text itself was not recovered from the current SFDA repository, so it is deliberately excluded from the verbatim-record set. Current MDS-REQ1 instead expressly bases the MDMA framework on the Medical Devices Law issued by Royal Decree M/54 and its Implementing Regulation. citeturn35search0turn35search1turn31view0

Taken together, these provisions produce a practical Saudi classification boundary for AI-driven CT. A recommendation-only tool used for diagnosis or therapy falls within Rule 11 once it meets the medical-device definition; a system that actually drives or influences the CT scanner and has a medical purpose is expressly brought into SaMD regulation by MDS-G027; and equipment/software falling within the category that controls, monitors, or directly influences ionizing-radiation diagnostic equipment engages the Class C language in Rule 10. Risk rises further where a CDS error can cause serious deterioration, surgery, death, or irreversible deterioration. citeturn26view0turn32view0

SFDA’s AI guidance is also directly relevant to automation bias. It calls for evaluation of human-computer interaction, user trust and behavior, and whether interface design introduces bias or unduly influences the user; the newer MDS-G027 expressly requires safeguards for human review/intervention. These are **device-risk-management requirements, not a statutory allocation of clinical malpractice liability**. citeturn28view0turn27view0

### Renal threshold map

The current **ACR 2026** renal-risk logic that can be verified from primary text is therefore:

| Renal status | Verified current ACR implication |
|---|---|
| Stable eGFR ≥45 mL/min/1.73 m² | IV iodinated contrast is not considered an independent nephrotoxic risk in the cited evidence base. citeturn22view1 |
| Stable eGFR 30–44 | Contrast is described as either non-nephrotoxic or only rarely nephrotoxic; routine prophylaxis is not mandated, but prophylaxis may be considered individually when additional high-risk circumstances exist. citeturn22view1turn22view2 |
| AKI or eGFR <30 | Prophylactic volume expansion is indicated unless the risk of fluids, such as heart failure/hypervolemia, outweighs benefit. citeturn22view2turn23view0 |
| Metformin, no AKI, eGFR ≥30 | ACR does not require stopping metformin for ordinary IV iodinated contrast and does not require obligatory post-procedure renal reassessment solely for metformin. citeturn22view3 |
| Metformin with AKI or eGFR <30 | Stop at/before contrast, withhold for 48 hours, then reassess renal function before restarting; the same conservative pathway applies to specified arterial catheter procedures carrying renal embolic risk. citeturn23view3 |

ACR also describes isotonic saline as the preferred prophylactic fluid and gives typical approaches that include fixed-volume regimens or approximately 1–3 mL/kg/hour, with longer regimens generally providing more protection but often being operationally impractical. citeturn23view0

A **current universal ACR renal-safety rule such as “contrast volume must not exceed X mL/kg” was not identified** in the reviewed renal chapter. The explicit contrast-volume-to-renal-function ratio located in primary source text came from the older ESUR 10.0 document, not the current 2025 capture, so it should not be silently implemented as a current Saudi or current ESUR statutory rule. citeturn41search3turn43view0

No qualifying public Saudi MoH source was located specifying national pre-CT creatinine timing, eGFR cut-offs, saline/bicarbonate prophylaxis, metformin hold/restart rules, or a renal-function-based contrast-volume formula. Thus:

**Saudi MoH eGFR 30 threshold — UNVERIFIED.**  
**Saudi MoH eGFR 45 threshold — UNVERIFIED.**  
**Saudi national metformin/iodinated-contrast rule — UNVERIFIED.**  
**Saudi national weight- or eGFR-based iodine-volume ceiling — UNVERIFIED.**

The consolidated PDPL treats health information as protected personal data and places Health Data within the sensitive-data regime. The Implementing Regulation substantially strengthens that position: processing Sensitive Data triggers impact-assessment obligations; legitimate-interest processing cannot simply be used as a basis for Sensitive Data; and Article 26 imposes health-specific organizational, technical, administrative, role-separation, traceability, processor-contract, and data-minimization controls. citeturn36search0turn38view0

Article 26 is particularly important for medical AI because it requires documented responsibility across stages of Health Data processing, differentiated access and duties, and incorporation of requirements issued by the MoH, Saudi Health Council, Council of Health Insurance and other competent health-sector bodies. In an AI radiology pipeline, that reaches well beyond model training: ingestion of CT metadata, laboratory renal data, contrast history, AI inference inputs/outputs, and audit trails can all constitute processing activities where identifiable health data are involved. The latter application is an inference from Article 26’s processing controls rather than a CT-specific statutory clause. citeturn37view0

Saudi data-protection law does directly recognize **solely automated decision-making**, but it does so primarily as a data-processing and transparency/consent issue. The Implementing Regulation requires disclosure of whether decisions will be made solely by automated processing, requires explicit consent where decisions are made solely that way when consent is the applicable basis, and makes automated decision-making/new technology a DPIA trigger in the circumstances described by Article 25. citeturn38view0turn37view0

What it does **not** do in the provisions reviewed is assign the clinical negligence burden between a radiologist, physician, technologist, hospital, AI manufacturer, and software deployer when an algorithmic recommendation harms a patient. Data-controller/processor accountability exists, including direct accountability where a processor departs from controller instructions, but that is not equivalent to a clinical-malpractice rule governing an incorrect AI-generated contrast dose or CT protocol. citeturn37view0

For interoperability, an official Saudi NPHIES implementation guide describes NPHIES as a centralized, validating, standards-based information-exchange gateway connecting Saudi providers and payers. That is relevant infrastructure, but it is **not a substitute for an NHIC clinical HIE policy**, and no directly qualifying NHIC policy text governing AI-CDS exchange, CT protocol data, or algorithm recommendations was located in the primary-source search. citeturn40search2

WHO’s six-principle framework is broader than ordinary medical-device performance regulation. In full, the principles address protection of human autonomy; human well-being, safety and public interest; transparency/explainability/intelligibility; responsibility/accountability; inclusiveness/equity; and responsive, sustainable AI. citeturn41search0turn41search4

The human-oversight issue is central to the WHO approach. WHO’s preparatory governance work explicitly warns that clinicians can display **automation bias**, accepting or over-weighting automated outputs rather than independently evaluating whether the technology is appropriate to the clinical situation. That problem is directly relevant to AI-selected CT parameters and contrast recommendations because the danger is not limited to a technically inaccurate model; it also includes a correct-looking output being adopted uncritically by a human operator. citeturn41search9

WHO’s LMM guidance is applicable when a system uses or generates combinations of text, medical images, clinical records, laboratory values, or other modalities rather than functioning as a narrowly bounded image classifier. For an oncology CT assistant that ingests images, eGFR, medication data, prior examinations, and free-text clinical context, the LMM governance framework is therefore potentially relevant in addition to device regulation; whether a particular product is legally an LMM or SaMD still depends on its actual architecture and intended use. citeturn41search1turn41search7

The ITU/WHO FG-AI4H operated from 2018 until September 2023 and was expressly designed to develop common assessment methodology for AI used in health diagnosis, triage, and treatment decisions. Its publication set includes ethics and governance, regulatory considerations, IMDRF mapping, software lifecycle, data requirements and annotation, training/test data, AI-training practices, technical testing, clinical evaluation, and domain use cases including radiology. citeturn41search2turn41search5turn41search23

## REGULATORY GAPS

**AI recommendation accepted by a fatigued technologist.** No explicit Saudi statutory provision was located that answers the specific liability question: *who bears clinical liability when an AI-generated CT protocol or iodinated-contrast recommendation is accepted by a fatigued technologist and causes harm?* SFDA does regulate the upstream risk: its AI guidance requires examination of human-computer interaction, user trust and behavior, possible undue influence by interface design, human oversight, and hand-off concepts for autonomous systems; MDS-G027 likewise requires safeguards for human review/intervention. But the reviewed texts do not turn those engineering controls into a rule saying liability transfers to the manufacturer, remains with the technologist, rests with the supervising physician, or is shared with the healthcare institution. citeturn28view0turn27view0

**No Saudi AI-specific standard of care for contrast dosing.** No primary Saudi text was located prescribing how an AI may calculate iodinated contrast dose from body weight, lean body mass, iodine delivery rate, eGFR, cardiac output, oncologic indication, or scanner characteristics. Current ACR renal thresholds can support a hospital’s clinical governance, but they are external professional guidance rather than Saudi statutory thresholds. citeturn22view1turn22view2

**No verified national Saudi eGFR 30/45 rule.** The values **30** and **45 mL/min/1.73 m²** are strongly traceable to international contrast literature and current ACR risk stratification, but no qualifying Saudi MoH primary protocol containing those cut-offs was found. Therefore hard-coding “30” or “45” into a Saudi AI rule engine as a purported *Saudi regulatory mandate* would overstate the evidence assembled here. The safer governance formulation is to identify the threshold explicitly as an adopted institutional/ACR/ESUR clinical rule and preserve the source/version in the algorithm’s rule provenance. citeturn22view1turn22view2

**No verified current Saudi CT DRL values in this capture.** MDS-G008 is documented as a Saudi National Diagnostic Reference Levels instrument, but the primary SFDA PDF and its CT tables were not successfully recovered. Numeric Saudi CT DRLs are therefore **UNVERIFIED here** and intentionally omitted rather than copied from secondary literature. citeturn42search2

**No oncology-specific cumulative CT ceiling found.** No Saudi primary provision located in this research establishes a cumulative lifetime CT-dose ceiling or fixed surveillance interval for repeatedly imaged cancer patients. This is materially different from the general requirement to justify and optimize each medical exposure; an AI scheduler should not invent a statutory cumulative-dose prohibition where none has been verified. International IAEA/ICRP principles support procedure-level justification and optimization, but they do not by themselves create a Saudi oncology-specific numeric limit. citeturn40search11turn42search14

**Autonomous scanner control is regulated more clearly than autonomous clinical accountability.** SFDA provides a reasonably clear device-classification pathway for software that drives/influences hardware, and Rule 10 expressly reaches systems controlling, monitoring, or directly influencing diagnostic ionizing-radiation equipment. What is not explicit in the reviewed Saudi texts is the operational governance layer: mandatory physician confirmation before AI changes kVp/mAs/pitch/scan range, mandatory double-checks for contrast dose, permissible autonomous bounds, override logging, responsibility after ignored alerts, or responsibility when workload/fatigue defeats the intended “human-in-the-loop” safeguard. citeturn26view0turn32view0turn27view0

**Automated-decision data law is not medical-negligence law.** The PDPL Implementing Regulation requires transparency around solely automated decisions, explicit consent in the specified automated-processing context, DPIAs for automated/new-technology processing in the stated circumstances, and detailed controls over Health Data. None of those provisions expressly determines whether a radiologist or technologist may legally delegate a clinical judgment to AI or who pays damages when that judgment is wrong. citeturn38view0turn37view0

**NHIC clinical-interoperability governance was not primary-verified.** An official NPHIES implementation guide was located and confirms a national standards-based exchange infrastructure for provider/payer transactions, but a qualifying direct NHIC policy governing exchange of AI recommendations, CT acquisition parameters, eGFR data, contrast decisions, algorithm provenance, or CDS audit events was not located. NPHIES should therefore not be cited as though it resolves the missing NHIC clinical-AI governance layer. citeturn40search2

**Historical Interim Regulation source gap.** SFDA primary material confirms the existence and Board-decree provenance of the Medical Devices Interim Regulation, but the actual historical regulatory text was not recovered from SFDA’s current public repository. Current MDMA practice should therefore be grounded in the Medical Devices Law/Implementing Regulation and current MDS-REQ1/MDS-G027/MDS-G010 materials unless the historical regulation itself is independently retrieved for a specific legacy question. citeturn35search0turn31view0
